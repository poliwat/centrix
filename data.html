The evidence

Three declines that don't care who's in office

Left and right argue endlessly over social questions. Meanwhile, the measures that decide whether an ordinary American can build a good life have moved the wrong way — across Democratic and Republican administrations alike. Here is the data, with sources. Judge for yourself.

Decline No. 1 — A roof of your own

Homes went from ~3× income to ~5×

In 1970 a typical home cost around three times the median household income — a level generations treated as normal. Today it sits near five times income, and a median-earning household would spend roughly 42% of income on the median home, far above the 30% affordability threshold.

Median home price ÷ median household income. Figures approximate, drawn from: Visual Capitalist, Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies, and Zoocasa. Coastal metros run far higher (8–12×); much of the Midwest and South runs lower (2.5–3.5×).

Presidents of both parties held office across this entire climb. Housing didn't get unaffordable on one side's watch — it got unaffordable on everyone's.
Decline No. 2 — The war on the middle class

Pay stopped tracking the work

For decades after World War II, worker pay rose in lockstep with productivity. Since 1979 they split apart: net productivity has grown about 90% while the pay of a typical worker grew only about 33% — productivity climbing roughly 3.5× as fast as pay. Had pay kept pace, the typical worker would earn on the order of $16 more per hour today.

Cumulative % growth since 1979, net productivity vs. typical worker compensation. Source: Economic Policy Institute, The Productivity–Pay Gap.

The gap opened under administrations of both parties and kept widening through boom and bust. The reward for work came unhooked from the work itself — and stayed that way regardless of who won.
Decline No. 3 — The cost of building a life

The essentials outran wages and inflation

Since 1980, the prices of the things that build a middle-class life have far outpaced general inflation. College tuition rose about 1,200% and healthcare about 450%, while the overall Consumer Price Index rose about 236%. Over a longer horizon, the dollar has lost more than 96% of its 1913 purchasing power.

Approximate cumulative price growth, 1980–2023. Sources: Forbes Advisor and BLS data (tuition, healthcare, CPI, housing). Figures rounded for comparison.

Every one of these curves climbed across both parties' time in power. The path into the middle class kept getting more expensive no matter who was steering.

Different sides. Same decline.

You can disagree with us about a hundred things and still see it: on the measures that matter most for an ordinary family, we've been losing ground together. That's the problem Centrix exists to solve.
