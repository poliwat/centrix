CENTRIX

Economic principles

Eight claims about how money actually behaves in a community — and the honest limit of each one.

With Saint Columba of Iona

Centrix argues for local production. That argument is only worth anything if the economics underneath it hold up, so this page sets them out plainly enough to be checked and disagreed with.

Every principle here is paired with its catch — the condition under which it stops being true. A framework that only lists its own strengths is advertising, not thinking. If you find one of these claims wrong, that is more useful to us than agreement.

On the second name. Much of what follows came out of a long argument with a friend who has spent decades in business, once looked seriously at running for Congress, and decided against it when he learned the only question anyone asked was how much money he could raise. He writes here as Saint Columba of Iona — a man who left home in middle age for a foreign country, and spent his later years advising and mediating between quarrelling kings without ever wanting a throne. His own clan were for generations the hereditary abbots of Columba's monastery, and carry the saint's hand and cross on their arms still.

He disagrees with parts of Centrix, and the disagreement improved it. Where he pushed back hardest, we have printed his objection rather than smoothed it over — see The strongest objection below.

The spine

Principle 1

Every dollar has a direction

Money does not simply get spent. It moves, repeatedly, and where it moves next is decided by who received it.

Spend a dollar at a business owned by someone who lives near you and it tends to move again nearby: to their staff, their suppliers, their accountant, their landlord. Spend the same dollar at a business headquartered elsewhere and most of it makes one local stop — a wage, perhaps a lease — before leaving for good.

The measured difference is large. Across studies by Civic Economics comparing independents with chains:

  • About 52.9% of a purchase at a local independent recirculates locally, against under 13.6% at a chain — roughly three times as much.
  • The Institute for Local Self-Reliance found $100 spent at Maine independents generated $45 of further local spending, against $14 at a big-box store.
  • Restaurants and service businesses recirculate more than retail, because more of their revenue goes straight into local wages.

But the inverse is the real argument, and it is the one Centrix cares about. If a local dollar multiplies locally, then a dollar that leaves multiplies somewhere else. It is not neutral and it does not vanish — it becomes someone else's payroll, someone else's tax base, someone else's town getting slightly better while yours gets slightly worse. Extraction is not the absence of circulation. It is circulation pointed away from you.

This is why the Production Assessment measures what a community is missing rather than what it has. A category your area cannot supply is a category your neighbours are funding elsewhere, every week, without deciding to.

The catch

These multipliers are frequently exaggerated. You will see claims that a local dollar is worth ten or a hundred times its face value — that is not what the research says and repeating it will cost you credibility with anyone who checks. The honest figure is that independents recirculate roughly three times as much as chains, and that a local-business economy produces a multiplier near 1.4 where a non-local one produces about 0.4. Meaningful, not magical. Note too that 53 cents of a dollar does not "stay" locally — it is the total generated across several rounds of spending.

Sources: AMIBA — The Local Multiplier Effect, summarising Civic Economics and ILSR studies; multiplier model from Shuman & Poole, Growing Local Living Economies (BALLE, 2012).

How a small producer actually competes

Principle 2

Scale wins on price and loses on nearly everything else

You will not out-price a corporation. You do not have to, because price is only one of the things people buy.

A national retailer buys in volumes you cannot approach and will always sell the commodity version cheaper. Competing there is a losing position, and any plan that depends on winning it should be abandoned early.

What scale cannot easily do is anything that resists standardisation: goods too perishable to ship, work that must be measured to a specific house, problems that need solving this afternoon, jobs where the customer wants one accountable person rather than a call centre, volumes too small to justify a production run. These are not consolation prizes. They are structurally defensible, because the cost advantages of scale turn into disadvantages the moment a job stops being identical every time.

The catch

"Better quality" is not by itself a strategy — plenty of excellent producers have gone broke while a worse competitor sold more. The advantage has to be something the customer notices before purchase and is willing to pay for at the moment of deciding. If you cannot name that thing in one sentence, you do not yet have one.

Principle 3

Overhead, not revenue, decides whether you survive

Low fixed costs are not frugality. They are the thing that lets you keep operating when revenue is bad, and they are the closest thing to freedom a small producer has.

Two businesses earning identically can face completely different situations. The one carrying a lease, financed equipment, and staff needs a good month every month. The one working out of a garage with paid-off tools can absorb a terrible quarter and continue. In a downturn the second business does not merely survive longer — it buys the first one's equipment cheaply.

This is the most transferable lesson from communities like the Amish, and it has little to do with technology or aesthetics. It is that a low cost base lets you accept smaller, steadier, more local work that a higher-overhead competitor cannot afford to take.

The catch

Low overhead is often achieved by quietly not counting your own labour. A garden that "saves a thousand dollars in vegetables" may represent two hundred hours — which is a fine trade if you wanted those hours, and a bad business if you did not. Price your time honestly even when nobody is invoicing you for it. Low overhead should mean few fixed obligations, not unpaid work.

Principle 4

Reputation is capital, and it compounds fastest where you are known

In a small market, being trusted is a durable asset that cannot be bought, hired, or transferred in.

A competitor can match your equipment within a week and your prices within a day. Twenty years of people saying you do what you said you would takes twenty years. This asset accrues faster in small communities precisely because information travels by conversation there — which is also why it is the one form of capital an outside firm entering your area cannot bring with it.

The catch

It is asymmetric and unforgiving. Reputation accumulates slowly and collapses quickly, and in a small market there is nowhere to restart. The same density of conversation that builds it in five years destroys it in an afternoon. Treat it as the collateral it is.

Discipline

Principle 5

Demand must be verified before it is served

The market is indifferent to what a thing cost you to make. Prove somebody will buy before you build the capacity to supply.

Consider hops. Demand is fairly inelastic — brewers need roughly what they need — while supply expands eagerly whenever prices rise. Growers plant into a high price, the crop lands as a glut, and then they hold out for a price the market has stopped offering. Nobody pays more because the harvest was expensive.

The practical form of this principle is unglamorous: presell. Take a deposit, fill one order, get a single paying customer before buying equipment. A verified customer is worth more than any market analysis, including ours.

The catch

This cuts against the data on our own assessment. A statistical gap is a hypothesis, not a market. Census figures exclude sole traders entirely, so a category that looks empty may already be served by people working from home. Go and look before you spend.

Principle 6

The openings that remain are the ones too small to interest capital

Obvious, large, easily captured opportunities are already taken. What is left is real, but it is small — and small is exactly what an individual can work with.

Money is good at finding cracks. If a straightforward way existed to make several million dollars supplying a market of nine thousand people, somebody would have done it, and the honest conclusion is that no such opening is sitting unclaimed.

But that argument has a floor. A company needing tens of millions in revenue cannot justify pursuing forty thousand a year — not because it is unprofitable, but because it is unprofitable at their cost structure, and the management attention alone would exceed the return. That is not a market failure. It is a permanent structural gap, and it is where nearly every viable local business lives. The opportunity is not hidden. It is merely beneath the threshold at which anyone large is willing to bend down.

The catch

Accept the implication. These are jobs, trades, and modest businesses — not ventures that scale into something large. If your goal is rapid growth into a big company, this framework is the wrong one and you should say so early rather than discover it in year three.

Independence

Principle 7

Not every exchange requires money

Shared tools, shared capacity, and mutual work reduce the amount of cash a producer must raise before starting.

The largest barrier for most small producers is capital equipment used intermittently. A kiln, a trailer, a commercial mixer, a bandsaw — each sits idle most of the week. Shared ownership, cooperative purchasing, tool libraries, and straightforward reciprocity between neighbours convert a purchase you cannot afford into an arrangement you can. This is the other half of the Amish lesson: not austerity, but the fact that shared capital equipment lets people start with far less money than the equipment costs.

The catch

Two warnings. Informal arrangements fail over unwritten expectations — agree in advance who repairs the thing, who insures it, and what happens if someone leaves. And barter is not a tax loophole: in the United States the fair market value of bartered goods and services is generally taxable income. Share to lower your capital requirement, not to hide revenue.

Principle 8

You do not need permission to begin

Policy could make local production dramatically easier. Waiting for it is optional, and probably unwise.

There is a real legislative agenda that would help: preferential tax treatment for genuinely local producers, eligibility for public food programmes, procurement rules favouring local supply, relief on utilities and inputs. Centrix supports arguments of that kind. But every one of them requires persuading people whose incentives point elsewhere, on a timescale measured in years.

Meanwhile nothing prevents a person from identifying an unmet need this month and filling it. The gap does not require a statute to exist, and closing it does not require one either. Policy changes the slope of the hill. It does not decide whether you walk up it.

The catch

Cutting the other way: regulation is sometimes the actual reason a gap exists. Licensing, zoning, cottage-food limits, and inspection regimes are frequently why nobody local supplies a thing. Find out which rules apply to you before assuming the field is open — an unserved market is occasionally unserved for a reason written down somewhere.

The strongest objection

People vote with their wallet, and they are broke

The most serious argument against everything above is not ideological, and it was put to us by Saint Columba of Iona, who does not accept the conclusions of this page:

When money is tight, people do not pay more to support local. They buy the cheap imported version at the big-box store, because that is what they can afford. Local producers cannot match the economies of scale that supply those shelves.

This is correct, and any version of localism that depends on shoppers accepting higher prices out of civic feeling will fail. Virtue is not a business model, and farmers' markets remain a minority channel largely because they are priced for the minority who can choose on principle.

Centrix draws two conclusions rather than arguing with it. The first is that local production has to compete on the customer's actual terms — price, convenience, speed, reliability — and not on where the money ends up. Circulation is why the outcome matters to a community. It is not a reason for anyone to pay a premium, and should never be used as one.

The second is that this objection defines the target. It rules out commodities where scale decides and leaves precisely the ground described in Principles 2 and 6: markets too small, too perishable, too custom, or too urgent for a distant supplier to serve well. Someone who mows lawns is not competing with global agriculture. Someone growing vegetables in neighbourhood yards and selling them on that street has no shipping cost, no distribution margin, and no spoilage in transit — and can sometimes undercut the supermarket rather than asking anyone to pay extra.

The test is therefore not whether buying local is admirable. It is whether a particular local offer is cheaper, faster, better, or easier than the alternative for the person deciding. Where it is, no persuasion is needed. Where it is not, no amount of persuasion will hold.

Who this is for

Honesty about the audience: most people who read this will do nothing with it, and that is not a character flaw. Producing something is harder than buying something. It costs evenings, it risks money, and it can fail publicly in front of people you know. Plenty of people have entirely sound reasons to decline — a job they value, children to raise, a parent to care for, health that will not allow it, or a life that is already full. Nothing here is an accusation aimed at them.

But the two positions are not symmetrical, and it is worth saying plainly. A consumer's position is interchangeable: whatever you bought, somebody else could have bought, and the seller is indifferent as to which of you it was. A producer's position is not interchangeable. If you are the only person within thirty miles who does a particular thing well, that is not a moral achievement — it is leverage, and leverage is what lets a person set terms rather than accept them. Consuming spends a position. Producing builds one.

Which is the honest reason to prefer it. Not because producers are better people, but because a country where more people hold a position of their own is harder to squeeze — and the three declines Centrix exists to address are, in the end, a long story about ordinary people running out of leverage.

So Centrix is not a campaign to convert a nation of consumers. It is a set of tools for the minority who already want out and cannot yet see the door. If that is not you, these principles still explain where your money goes when you spend it, which is worth knowing on its own. If it is you, the assessment is the next step.

What we are not claiming

  • That buying local fixes wage stagnation, housing costs, or the price of healthcare. It does not. Those need policy.
  • That a local dollar is worth ten or a hundred times its face value. It is worth roughly three times a chain dollar in local recirculation.
  • That most small producers succeed. Most do not, and the ones who do usually started smaller than they wanted to.
  • That local production scales into a large company. Usually it will not, by the logic of Principle 6.
  • That any of this is fast. Reputation compounds over years, which is the point of it.

These principles are the reasoning behind the Production Assessment, which applies them to your own ZIP code using public Census data.
